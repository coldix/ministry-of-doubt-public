# The Ministry of Doubt Truth Protocol

**Working name:** **The Trivium+ Protocol**  
**Status:** experimental method — test against case dossiers before promoting to canonical method  
**Purpose:** turn the Ministry's central principle — *Suspicion is not proof. Authority is not proof either. Show me the evidence.* — into a repeatable procedure for deciding what a claim has actually earned.

---

# 1. The central idea

The Ministry should not attempt to become a new authority that tells readers what to believe.

It should expose a method by which a reader can see:

1. exactly what claim is being tested;
2. what evidence exists for and against it;
3. where that evidence came from;
4. how independent the evidentiary lines really are;
5. what competing explanations predict;
6. which evidence actually discriminates between them;
7. where incentives, rhetoric, censorship, prestige or group pressure may have distorted the process;
8. what would change the assessment;
9. and how the conclusion changed as new evidence arrived.

The method is deliberately harder on a preferred theory than on a theory nobody cares about.

> **The object is not to win an argument. The object is to make it increasingly difficult to remain wrong.**

---

# 2. Philosophical foundation: realism without dogma

The method borrows useful elements from Aristotelian logic and Objectivist epistemology without making Objectivism itself an authority.

## 2.1 Reality is prior to belief

A proposition does not become true because:

- I believe it;
- most people believe it;
- experts believe it;
- a government declares it;
- a dissident declares it;
- it is peer reviewed;
- it is censored;
- it goes viral;
- or it fits a compelling story.

Reality is what eventually has veto power.

This is the useful Objectivist starting point: facts do not alter to accommodate wishes, status or consensus.

## 2.2 Identity matters

Before asking whether a statement is true, determine what the statement actually means.

A thing is what it is. A claim cannot slide between definitions halfway through an argument.

Examples:

- "interference" is not automatically "collusion";
- "association" is not "complicity";
- "unsafe" is not the same claim as "not proven safe";
- "possible" is not "probable";
- "a flawed investigation" is not "the opposite conclusion is therefore true".

## 2.3 Contradictions are diagnostic, not magical

Two genuinely contradictory propositions cannot both be true in the same sense at the same time.

But finding a contradiction does **not** tell us automatically which proposition is false.

A contradiction is a research target.

## 2.4 Human knowledge is fallible

This is where the Ministry must be more explicitly probabilistic than some forms of philosophical certainty.

Reality may be definite while our access to it is partial, noisy, manipulated or incomplete.

Therefore empirical conclusions are usually held with degrees of confidence and must remain revisable.

> **Objective reality does not imply omniscient observers.**

## 2.5 No philosophy is exempt

Objectivism, Bayesian reasoning, Popperian falsification, mainstream science, contrarian science and the Ministry's own rules are all subject to the same test:

**Do they help us make better contact with reality?**

If a rule repeatedly protects preferred conclusions from contrary evidence, discard or modify the rule.

---

# 3. The Trivium becomes a research workflow

The classical Trivium gives the Ministry a useful three-stage cognitive sequence:

1. **Grammar — What are we actually talking about?**
2. **Logic — What follows from what?**
3. **Rhetoric — How is the conclusion being sold to us?**

The Ministry adds four further stages:

4. **Reality test — What observations discriminate between competing explanations?**
5. **Independence audit — How many evidentiary lines do we really have?**
6. **Adversarial review — Can competent opponents break the argument?**
7. **Update — What is now justified, and what remains unknown?**

This is the **Trivium+ Protocol**.

---

# 4. Stage Zero — Pre-register before the hunt

Before deep research, write down the starting position.

This already exists in the Ministry case template and should remain mandatory.

Record:

- the exact claims;
- current confidence;
- why that confidence exists;
- strongest conventional explanation;
- strongest alternative explanation;
- evidence expected if each explanation is true;
- evidence that would weaken the preferred view;
- evidence that would strengthen it;
- known conflicts or emotional stakes;
- sources not yet examined.

Commit this block before harvesting evidence wherever practical.

## Why this matters

Humans are extremely good at remembering that they "always thought" whatever the evidence eventually supports.

Git history makes that harder.

The pre-registration is not a vow never to change. It is evidence that the change was real.

> **Changing your mind is not a methodological failure. Hiding that you changed it is.**

---

# 5. GRAMMAR — Define the claim before testing it

Most bad arguments begin before the evidence stage because the proposition itself is vague, overloaded or moving.

## 5.1 Atomise the claim

Break large narratives into propositions small enough to test.

Do not research:

> "9/11 was an inside job."

Research separately:

- Who carried out the attacks?
- What did particular agencies know before the attacks?
- Were warnings mishandled?
- How did each building fail?
- Did any actor knowingly conceal relevant evidence afterwards?
- How was the crisis used politically afterwards?

Likewise, do not research "the 2020 election was stolen" as one object. Break it into specific claims by jurisdiction, mechanism, magnitude and evidence.

## 5.2 Classify the proposition

A claim may be:

- descriptive — *X happened*;
- quantitative — *X occurred at this magnitude*;
- causal — *X caused Y*;
- intentional — *A intended Y*;
- conspiratorial — *A and B secretly coordinated to produce Y*;
- predictive — *X will happen*;
- counterfactual — *without X, Y would not have happened*;
- normative — *X should happen*.

Different claim types require different evidence.

A document may establish an intention without establishing execution. A correlation may establish association without establishing cause. A suspicious pattern may establish a question without identifying an actor.

## 5.3 Define loaded terms

For every disputed term ask:

- What does it mean here?
- What observations would qualify?
- What observations would not qualify?
- Has either side changed the definition during the argument?

Terms requiring special caution include:

`conspiracy`, `cover-up`, `safe`, `effective`, `fraud`, `rigged`, `censored`, `collusion`, `misinformation`, `expert`, `consensus`, `peer reviewed`, `proven`, `debunked`, `hoax`, `terrorism`, `genocide`, `racism`, `corruption`.

The definition should narrow the dispute, not predetermine the verdict.

## 5.4 Build a claim tree

Each major case should have a claim tree:

```text
Main proposition
├── C1 factual subclaim
│   ├── C1.1 supporting factual claim
│   └── C1.2 supporting factual claim
├── C2 causal subclaim
└── C3 intent / coordination subclaim
```

A parent claim cannot be stronger than the unsupported subclaims on which it depends.

---

# 6. LOGIC — Test the inference, not merely the facts

True facts can be assembled into a false argument.

Logic asks whether the conclusion actually follows.

## 6.1 Write competing hypotheses

Never allow a controversial case to collapse into a binary choice too early.

As a default, consider at least:

- **H1 — conventional/institutional explanation**;
- **H2 — strongest serious alternative**;
- **H3 — error, incompetence, capture, incentives or emergent coordination without the alleged conspiracy**;
- **H4 — mixed explanation or presently unknown**.

Some cases will need more. Some will need fewer.

The purpose is to prevent the common mistake:

> "The official story has a hole, therefore my story wins."

A damaged hypothesis does not automatically transfer its probability to one preferred rival.

## 6.2 Ask the most important question in the method

For every important evidence item **E**, ask:

> **Would I be substantially more likely to observe E if H1 were true, or if H2 were true?**

This is the qualitative form of a likelihood-ratio test.

Evidence can be:

- strongly discriminating;
- moderately discriminating;
- weakly discriminating;
- non-discriminating;
- or presently uninterpretable.

A dramatic fact is not necessarily a discriminating fact.

Example:

A government benefiting politically from a crisis is compatible with:

- creating the crisis;
- anticipating and exploiting the crisis;
- opportunistically exploiting an unexpected crisis.

Benefit alone therefore has little power to distinguish creation from exploitation.

## 6.3 Separate necessary from sufficient evidence

If a theory requires secret coordination, evidence of coordination may be necessary.

But evidence that two people met may not be sufficient to establish what they coordinated.

Make the distinction explicit.

## 6.4 Audit hidden premises

For each inference write the missing sentence required to make the argument valid.

Example:

> "The records were destroyed, therefore the accused allegation is true."

Hidden premise:

> "The only plausible reason to destroy the records was to conceal the specific alleged act."

Now the hidden premise can be tested instead of smuggled through.

## 6.5 Standard logic traps

Flag at least the following:

- affirming the consequent;
- denying the antecedent;
- equivocation;
- false dichotomy;
- circular reasoning;
- selection bias;
- survivorship bias;
- base-rate neglect;
- post hoc causation;
- correlation treated as causation;
- motive treated as proof of action;
- opportunity treated as proof of action;
- association treated as complicity;
- credential treated as proof;
- dissent treated as proof;
- censorship treated as proof;
- absence of expected evidence treated as positive proof without modelling why it should exist;
- anomaly stacking;
- moving the goalposts;
- unfalsifiable escape clauses.

## 6.6 The anomaly rule

An unexplained anomaly is not worthless, but its value depends on whether it distinguishes explanations.

Ask:

1. How unlikely is the anomaly under the leading explanation?
2. How likely is it under the alternative?
3. Was it identified before or after searching thousands of possible anomalies?
4. Is there a denominator?
5. Can the observation itself be independently verified?

A theory that explains every anomaly after the fact may explain nothing before the fact.

# 7. RHETORIC — Strip the persuasion away from the proposition

Rhetoric is not automatically deception. Every author selects, orders and explains.

But presentation can create confidence the evidence does not deserve.

The Ministry therefore performs a **rhetorical audit** before accepting either side's framing.

## 7.1 Neutral rewrite

Rewrite the strongest claim without adjectives, tribal labels or conclusions embedded in the nouns.

Replace:

> "The corrupt regime desperately covered up the devastating evidence."

with something like:

> "The agency did not release document X after requests A, B and C. The stated reason was Y."

Then test the underlying facts.

## 7.2 Steelman before rebuttal

Each major case requires:

- the strongest fair statement of the institutional/conventional account;
- the strongest fair statement of the serious alternative.

A case has not been tested if one side is represented by its weakest internet advocate.

## 7.3 The identity-swap test

Ask:

> **If the same evidence supported the political, cultural or institutional side I dislike, would I give it the same weight?**

If not, identify why.

There may be a legitimate contextual reason. But it must be stated rather than assumed.

## 7.4 Separate social reaction from factual evidence

These may be important **process evidence**:

- censorship;
- demonetisation;
- media pile-ons;
- official talking points;
- coordinated messaging;
- intimidation;
- suppression of data;
- refusal to answer questions.

But they do not automatically prove the disputed **object-level claim**.

The process may deserve a severe verdict while the underlying event remains UNKNOWN.

## 7.5 Narrative coherence is not probability

Human beings prefer stories with agents, motives, turning points and closure.

Reality is under no obligation to be well written.

Bureaucratic confusion, overlapping motives, partial concealment, coincidence and incompetence can produce ugly stories with no single author.

---

# 8. REALITY TEST — Translate the scientific method to messy human events

Historical and institutional cases cannot always be repeated like laboratory experiments.

But the scientific discipline still applies.

## 8.1 Move from explanation to prediction

For each hypothesis ask:

> **If this explanation were true, what else should we expect to observe?**

Examples:

- documents;
- timing;
- financial flows;
- physical traces;
- communications;
- witness behaviour;
- independent measurements;
- geographic distributions;
- software logs;
- metadata;
- later admissions;
- predictable statistical patterns.

Write these predictions before hunting for them whenever possible.

## 8.2 Prefer risky predictions

A useful theory risks being wrong.

A weak theory predicts:

> "There will be anomalies."

A stronger theory predicts:

> "If mechanism X occurred, records of type Y should show pattern Z during period T and should not appear in control group Q."

The more specific the prediction, the more informative success or failure becomes.

## 8.3 Replicate analyses, not merely quotations

Where a claim depends on data:

- obtain the dataset where lawful and practical;
- preserve the raw version;
- record transformations;
- reproduce the calculation;
- test alternative reasonable assumptions;
- check sensitivity to exclusions and thresholds;
- publish code or calculation steps where possible.

A chart is an argument with many hidden premises.

## 8.4 Separate confirmatory from exploratory work

Exploration is legitimate. It is how patterns are discovered.

But an exploratory pattern found after looking through many possibilities should not be presented as though it was predicted in advance.

Label it:

- **pre-specified / confirmatory**, or
- **post-hoc / exploratory**.

Then seek an independent test.

## 8.5 Use controls

Where applicable use:

- negative controls;
- positive controls;
- comparison jurisdictions;
- unaffected time periods;
- independent instruments;
- alternative datasets;
- blinded coding;
- sensitivity analysis.

The book itself should contain calibration controls:

- cases where institutions really did conspire or conceal;
- cases where institutional failure occurred without the alleged grand conspiracy;
- cases where anti-establishment claims fail under strong evidence;
- genuinely unresolved cases.

If the method always finds conspiracy, it is broken.

If the method never finds conspiracy, it is also broken.

---

# 9. INDEPENDENCE AUDIT — Count evidence lines, not headlines

The existing Ministry rule remains fundamental:

> **Twenty stories tracing to one unnamed source are one evidentiary line, not twenty.**

## 9.1 Build source lineage

For every major source ask:

- Is this primary or derivative?
- What is the earliest source I can find?
- Does this source rely on another source already counted?
- Did multiple outlets receive the same briefing?
- Are multiple experts analysing the same dataset with the same method?
- Are apparently independent witnesses actually communicating with one another?

Assign an `independence_group`.

## 9.2 Independence has several dimensions

Two sources may be independent in one sense and dependent in another.

Check:

- **data independence** — different underlying observations?
- **method independence** — different measurement or analytical method?
- **institutional independence** — different organisations?
- **financial independence** — different funding/control?
- **social independence** — formed judgement before discussion?
- **informational independence** — not merely repeating the same report?

The strongest convergence often comes from different methods reaching the same result.

## 9.3 Consensus is evidence, not a trump card

A genuine expert consensus can be important evidence because expertise contains information.

But the weight of consensus depends on how it formed.

Ask:

- How many genuinely independent evidentiary lines support it?
- Is the field working from the same small set of data?
- Are different methods converging?
- Can dissenting results be published and careers survive?
- Are important assumptions shared rather than independently tested?
- Is the consensus about the **data**, the **model**, or a **policy judgement**?
- Has the consensus survived serious prediction and replication?

A thousand experts repeating one model output are not a thousand measurements.

Conversely, the mere existence of dissent does not invalidate a consensus.

## 9.4 Conflicts alter burden, not truth value

Funding, career incentives, ideology, legal exposure, prestige and institutional loyalty may affect behaviour.

Record them.

Do not commit the genetic fallacy:

> "This study was funded by X, therefore it is false."

Instead ask whether the conflict increases the need for:

- independent replication;
- raw data;
- preregistration;
- transparent methods;
- or corroboration from differently incentivised actors.

# 10. PEER REVIEW — Useful filter, false certificate

The Ministry should reject two simplistic positions:

1. **Peer reviewed = true.**
2. **Peer review is corrupt, therefore peer-reviewed work is worthless.**

Neither follows.

Peer review is a human quality-control mechanism. It can improve work and detect errors, but it is subject to limited reviewer time, expertise, publication incentives, prestige effects, common assumptions and the same social pressures affecting other institutions.

The correct question is:

> **What exactly has this paper survived, and what has it not yet survived?**

## 10.1 Record more than the badge

For important scientific claims record where possible:

- peer-review status;
- review model if known;
- preregistration or Registered Report status;
- sample size and power;
- effect size and uncertainty;
- primary outcome versus secondary/exploratory outcomes;
- data availability;
- code availability;
- conflicts of interest;
- corrections;
- retractions;
- direct replications;
- conceptual replications;
- independent meta-analysis;
- post-publication criticism.

A paper's position in the evidence hierarchy can rise or fall after publication.

## 10.2 Replication outranks prestige

A famous journal cannot make a fragile result robust.

Independent successful replication, transparent data and converging methods should usually increase confidence more than journal prestige alone.

Failed replication should not automatically erase a result either. Examine power, protocol fidelity, population differences and the full replication record.

## 10.3 Borrow from Registered Reports

The Ministry's Git pre-registration is conceptually close to the strongest feature of Registered Reports: important methodological commitments are exposed **before** the result is known.

For data-heavy Ministry investigations, pre-register where practical:

- hypotheses;
- inclusion criteria;
- thresholds;
- statistical tests;
- exclusions;
- stopping rules;
- expected patterns.

Then label deviations openly.

# 11. GROUPTHINK — Independence before deliberation

Groupthink is not solved by replacing one tribe with another tribe.

A room full of contrarians can herd as effectively as a room full of establishment experts.

The Ministry should therefore engineer disagreement into the process.

## 11.1 Sealed first judgement

Before reviewers see one another's conclusions, each should record independently:

- claim status;
- confidence;
- strongest evidence for;
- strongest evidence against;
- most dangerous hidden assumption;
- evidence most likely to change the verdict.

Only then should reviewers discuss the case.

This preserves informational independence while still gaining the benefits of later argument.

## 11.2 Do not reveal the preferred answer early

Where feasible, reviewers should receive the claim, evidence bundle and task before being told the author's current verdict.

The question is not:

> "Can you find support for Colin's conclusion?"

It is:

> "Given this evidence, what conclusion is justified and why?"

## 11.3 Rotate the dissenter

For major cases nominate a reviewer whose explicit task is to defeat the leading conclusion.

This is a role, not an ideology.

On one case the dissenter may attack an institutional conclusion. On the next they may attack the conspiracy claim.

## 11.4 Seek methodological diversity

Three structural engineers using the same model are not equivalent to:

- a structural engineer;
- a fire engineer;
- a materials specialist;
- and an independent modeller using different assumptions.

Review diversity should follow the causal structure of the claim.

## 11.5 Preserve disagreement

Do not force artificial consensus.

If competent reviewers disagree after seeing the same evidence, that disagreement is itself information about uncertainty.

The public record can say:

> "Two reviewers regard C4 as PROBABLE; one regards it as POSSIBLE because assumption A remains unresolved."

That is more informative than pretending the review process produced unanimity.

---

# 12. THE MINISTRY'S ADVERSARIAL REVIEW MODEL

For load-bearing claims, replace ordinary "peer review" with **adversarial open review**.

## Round A — Independent review

At least three roles where practical:

1. **Domain specialist** — knows the subject matter.
2. **Methods specialist** — statistics, modelling, software, law, forensics or another relevant method.
3. **Hostile competent reader** — specifically tasked with challenging the leading conclusion.

Each submits a sealed first assessment.

## Round B — Cross-examination

Reviewers see:

- the author's reasoning;
- each other's objections;
- source lineage;
- unresolved contradictions.

They may revise their assessment, but the initial assessment remains preserved.

## Round C — Adjudication

The author records:

- which objections were accepted;
- which were rejected and why;
- which remain unresolved;
- whether the claim ledger changed;
- whether confidence changed;
- what evidence would resolve the remaining dispute.

The purpose is not for reviewers to certify truth.

The purpose is to document what the claim survived.

---

# 13. USING AI WITHOUT BUILDING A MACHINE CONSENSUS

AI can enormously improve the Ministry's adversarial process, but only if models are treated as analytical tools rather than evidence sources.

## 13.1 Models are reviewers, not witnesses

An AI answer does not count as an independent evidentiary line.

Every material factual claim generated by a model must trace to an inspectable source.

Five models repeating the same web article remain one evidentiary line.

## 13.2 Blind parallel review

For important dossiers:

1. give multiple models the same evidence bundle independently;
2. do not show them other model conclusions initially;
3. ask each for the strongest case for and against;
4. ask each what would falsify its preferred explanation;
5. compare disagreements;
6. only then run a synthesis pass.

This reduces anchoring and model-to-model imitation.

## 13.3 Use asymmetric roles deliberately

Example AI panel:

- Model A — conventional explanation steelman;
- Model B — alternative explanation steelman;
- Model C — methodological auditor with no preferred outcome;
- Model D — source-lineage and citation auditor;
- Synthesis — reconcile only after independent outputs are frozen.

Do not treat a 4–0 model vote as evidence. Inspect why they agree and whether they share training priors or sources.

---

# 14. EVIDENCE WEIGHTING — Use dimensions, not one magic score

Avoid collapsing evidence quality into a single pseudo-precise number.

For each major evidence item record these dimensions:

| Dimension | Question |
|---|---|
| **Directness** | Does it bear directly on the proposition or only indirectly? |
| **Provenance** | Can origin, custody and authenticity be established? |
| **Independence** | Does it add a genuinely new evidentiary line? |
| **Diagnosticity** | Does it discriminate between competing hypotheses? |
| **Reproducibility** | Can the observation or analysis be independently reproduced? |
| **Transparency** | Are data, method and assumptions inspectable? |
| **Adversarial survival** | Has it survived competent challenge? |
| **Sensitivity** | Does the conclusion collapse under reasonable alternative assumptions? |
| **Temporal proximity** | Was it recorded contemporaneously or reconstructed later? |
| **Conflict burden** | Are incentives strong enough to require extra corroboration? |

These dimensions guide judgement. They do not mechanically output truth.

# 15. THE TWO-LEDGER VERDICT

The master outline correctly separates **evidence status** from **confidence**. Keep that separation.

## 15.1 Claim Ledger

- **KNOWN** — strongly established by primary evidence or multiple genuinely independent high-quality lines;
- **PROBABLE** — best supported explanation, with meaningful uncertainty remaining;
- **POSSIBLE** — plausible and not excluded, but evidence insufficient to prefer strongly;
- **UNSUPPORTED** — asserted beyond present evidence;
- **UNKNOWN** — crucial evidence is missing or available evidence does not discriminate adequately.

## 15.2 Confidence

- Very high
- High
- Moderate
- Low
- Very low

A proposition may be PROBABLE with moderate confidence.

A proposition may be POSSIBLE with high confidence that it is possible.

UNKNOWN is not 50%.

## 15.3 Process and object-level remain separate

Each major case must carry two verdicts where relevant:

### Process

Did an institution or actor:

- lie;
- conceal;
- destroy;
- censor;
- mislead;
- act negligently;
- exceed authority;
- manipulate process;
- or fail transparently?

### Object level

What actually happened in the event under investigation?

A strong process finding may coexist with an UNKNOWN object-level result.

That is not an unsatisfying conclusion. It may be the most truthful one available.

# 16. SYMMETRY TESTS

Before publication, ask:

1. Would I accept this evidence if it favoured the other political tribe?
2. Would I dismiss this source conflict if the source supported my side?
3. Am I demanding primary evidence from opponents while accepting commentary from allies?
4. Have I treated censorship as evidence of truth in one case but as irrelevant in another?
5. Have I treated consensus as decisive when it helps me and corrupt when it does not?
6. Have I used "groupthink" as an explanation without positive evidence of group dynamics?
7. Have I used "conspiracy" where incentives and shared assumptions explain the same evidence?
8. Have I used "incompetence" to avoid documents showing intent?
9. Have I moved the required evidence after seeing the result?
10. What is the strongest piece of evidence I wish did not exist?

Question 10 should appear in every major dossier.

---

# 17. STOP RULES — Prevent research from becoming anomaly addiction

A case can consume infinite time if every unexplained detail creates three new rabbit holes.

Stop or freeze an assessment when:

- the principal claims have stable evidence classifications;
- new sources are mostly derivative of existing independence groups;
- new anomalies have low discriminating value;
- sensitivity tests no longer materially change the conclusion;
- the strongest opposing case has been addressed;
- remaining questions are explicitly logged as UNKNOWN;
- further investigation has low expected value unless a new evidence class appears.

Live Files reopen when material new evidence appears, not merely because discourse continues.

---

# 18. PUBLICATION STANDARD

A Ministry case should not merely give the verdict.

A reader should be able to inspect:

- exact claims;
- evidence cut-off date;
- pre-registration snapshot;
- source lineage;
- evidence for;
- evidence against;
- strongest alternative explanation;
- unresolved contradictions;
- process verdict;
- object-level verdict;
- confidence;
- Claim Ledger status;
- what would change the assessment;
- review objections;
- update history.

This is the difference between **trust us** and **check us**.

# 19. CALIBRATION PROGRAMME

The protocol should be tested on four classes of case.

## A. Positive control — documented wrongdoing

Examples:

- Northwoods;
- MKUltra;
- COINTELPRO;
- Robodebt process findings;
- Horizon institutional misconduct.

The method must be capable of reaching strong conclusions when the evidence warrants them.

## B. Negative control — attractive claim that fails

Examples already planned:

- Apollo hoax;
- Wakefield/MMR claims.

The method must be capable of rejecting anti-establishment claims.

## C. Mixed case

A case where serious institutional misconduct does not automatically establish the strongest alternative event theory.

JFK archival/process issues may provide this role.

## D. Live uncertainty

A question where evidence is still arriving and UNKNOWN or PROBABLE may be the most responsible status.

The protocol should behave sensibly in all four classes before being treated as the Ministry's canonical method.

# 20. MINIMUM CASE WORKFLOW

For practical use, every investigation follows this sequence:

```text
0. PRE-REGISTER
   Freeze prior, claims, steelmen and falsifiers.

1. GRAMMAR
   Define terms. Atomise claims. Build claim tree.

2. LOGIC
   Build competing hypotheses. Expose hidden premises.

3. RHETORIC
   Neutralise framing. Steelman both sides. Run symmetry test.

4. REALITY TEST
   Derive predictions. Seek discriminating evidence. Replicate analysis.

5. INDEPENDENCE AUDIT
   Trace source lineage. Count evidence groups, not repetitions.

6. ADVERSARIAL REVIEW
   Independent first judgements → discussion → preserved dissent.

7. VERDICT
   Claim Ledger + confidence + process/object split.

8. UPDATE
   Preserve old verdict. Record exactly what changed and why.
```

# 21. A one-page checklist

Before calling any controversial proposition KNOWN or PROBABLE:

- [ ] Can I state the claim in one sentence without loaded language?
- [ ] Have I separated process claims from object-level claims?
- [ ] Was my prior recorded before deep research?
- [ ] Have I written the strongest serious competing explanation?
- [ ] Have I identified what each hypothesis predicts?
- [ ] Does the key evidence actually discriminate between them?
- [ ] Have I checked the denominator behind anomalies?
- [ ] Have I traced sources to their earliest available origin?
- [ ] Have I counted independent evidence lines rather than citations?
- [ ] Have I examined important contrary evidence directly?
- [ ] Have I replicated any important quantitative analysis where practical?
- [ ] Have I separated exploratory findings from pre-specified tests?
- [ ] Have I recorded conflicts and incentives without treating them as automatic disproof?
- [ ] Have reviewers formed initial judgements independently?
- [ ] Has a competent hostile reader tried to break the claim?
- [ ] Have I preserved meaningful reviewer disagreement?
- [ ] Have I run the political/identity symmetry test?
- [ ] Can I name the strongest evidence I wish did not exist?
- [ ] Can I state what would change my mind?
- [ ] Is UNKNOWN still allowed to win?

If the last answer is no, the method has already failed.

# 22. Research basis and notes

These are not authorities that settle the protocol. They are useful evidence about why particular safeguards are included.

## Objectivist epistemology

The useful borrowed propositions are the primacy of reality, disciplined definitions, reason and rejection of final human authority in knowledge claims. The Ministry does **not** import Objectivist ethical or political conclusions into the evidence protocol.

- Ayn Rand Institute, Objectivity: https://courses.aynrand.org/lexicon/objectivity/
- Ayn Rand Institute, Reason: https://courses.aynrand.org/lexicon/Reason/
- Ayn Rand Institute, Introduction to Objectivist Epistemology: https://aynrand.org/novels/introduction-to-objectivist-epistemology/

## Reproducibility and peer review

A large 2026 replication project in the social and behavioural sciences attempted replications of 274 positive-result claims from 164 quantitative papers across 54 journals. 151 of 274 claims reproduced a statistically significant result in the original pattern. This does **not** imply that half of science is false. It does show why publication and peer review cannot reasonably be treated as truth certificates.

- Investigating the replicability of the social and behavioural sciences, Nature (2026): https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-10078-y

Registered Reports move methodological review before results are known. Empirical comparison has found stronger ratings for methodological and analytical rigour in Registered Reports than matched standard papers, supporting the Ministry's use of pre-registration as an anti-bias device.

- Soderberg et al., Initial evidence of research quality of registered reports compared with the standard publishing model, Nature Human Behaviour (2021): https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-021-01142-4

Ioannidis' influential 2005 paper remains useful for its treatment of power, multiplicity, analytic flexibility, prior probability and bias, but its famous title should not itself become a slogan or authority. The Ministry should also note published criticism of the mathematical argument.

- Ioannidis, Why Most Published Research Findings Are False, PLOS Medicine (2005): https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.0020124

## Group reasoning

Experimental work suggests the relationship between social interaction and accuracy is not simply "groups bad, independence good". Social learning can produce herding under some conditions, while structured small-group deliberation can improve collective accuracy. The protocol therefore uses **independent first judgement followed by deliberation**, rather than either isolated individuals or immediate consensus formation.

- Toyokawa, Whalen & Laland, Social learning strategies regulate the wisdom and madness of interactive crowds, Nature Human Behaviour (2019): https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-018-0518-x
- Navajas et al., Aggregated knowledge from a small number of debates outperforms the wisdom of large crowds, Nature Human Behaviour (2018): https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-017-0273-4
